One terminal, whole studio.

How one AI session plans, builds and ships, and how to talk to it.

In this lesson

  • What one AI session actually is, and why it can run a whole studio
  • The three sentences that took a course idea to a live web page with a real signup in about 40 minutes
  • Five habits for talking to your crew so it does what you meant
  • The trap that makes a signup form say "thanks" while sending nothing, and how to beat it

1. The whole studio is one conversation

You installed your crew in Lesson 1. This lesson is how to work with it.

Quantum Melanin Media is two people. The games, the apps, the videos, the music and the uploads are built by an AI crew: Claude Code, running in one terminal window.

One session is one long conversation. You talk to it in plain English. The difference from a chatbot is what happens next: it doesn't hand you instructions to go do yourself. It opens the files, runs the commands, drives a browser, does the work, and comes back with proof.

Keep three parts in your head, because the whole course is built on them:

  1. Your words. The ask, the notes, and the go-live.
  2. The crew's hands. Files, the terminal, a browser. This is where the work happens.
  3. The crew's notes. The rules you've taught it, saved so the next session starts smart. (That's Lesson 3.)

2. The real job: one sentence to a live page

The course page you found us on was built in this lesson's case study. Here is everything Rob typed, word for word:

Time (PT) Rob typed What came back
4:19 PM let's start the course waitlist page A finished page, tested, open in a browser on our machine for review. Not live.
4:54 PM push it live and add the home page card The page and a home page card live on quantummelaninmedia.com, checked from the live site.
5:01 PM The first real test signup lands in the inbox.
5:07 PM yes add it to both videos and IG The link added to two YouTube descriptions and posted on Instagram, each one reloaded and confirmed.

Three sentences. About 40 minutes from the first one to a live page with a real signup in the inbox, and that includes the time Rob spent looking it over.

Notice what Rob did not type: no file names, no code, no list of steps. He said what he wanted, looked at the result, and said when it could go public.

3. What happened between the sentences

This is the part you never see in a demo, and it's the part that makes it trustworthy. Between Rob's first and second sentence, the crew:

  • Read its notes first. Which website, how it deploys, which signup form was already proven to work. It didn't guess.
  • Reused what already works. Same look as the rest of the site, same form setup that was already delivering signups. New code only where something new was needed.
  • Tested the failures on purpose. What if the form service refuses? What if the internet drops? What if someone types a bad email? A bot? The page had to show an honest error every time and never say "you're on the list" when you're not.
  • Showed it before shipping it. The page opened in a browser for Rob to judge, and then the crew waited.

After "push it live," it:

  • Ran the site's own check and only pushed when it passed.
  • Asked the live site, not the report. It pulled every file back down from the public website and compared it to what it had built. Then it signed up for real and went to the inbox to find the email.

That last step is the habit to steal. "Deployed successfully" is a claim. The email in the inbox is proof.

4. Five habits for talking to your crew

1. Say the outcome, not the steps. let's start the course waitlist page worked because the crew already knew the site, the style and the tools. You know what you want; it knows the steps. A strong ask has three parts:

what you want + who it's for + what "done" looks like

2. Keep the go-live word separate. Building and shipping are two different sentences. push it live was its own message. Until you say it, nothing goes public. That one rule lets you move fast without being nervous.

3. Give notes like a director: where, what, and what right sounds like. Real notes from the same day, on a commercial and an explainer:

  • Its night 'shift ' he said night shrift...redo (the exact word, and what was wrong with it)
  • Yeah, but now he says it too slow... (the next note, on the next take)
  • Nailed it...!!!.. (tell it when it's right, too)
  • you see the part at a minute and 30 something seconds ... we got to just, you know, fix that section (a timestamp and a scope: fix this part, leave the rest alone)

Short, specific, and in your own words. Your eye and ear are the final check. When a tool says a line sounds fine and your ear says it doesn't, your ear wins.

4. Teach it once. also remember when we use quantus and tess to do explainers we use the voices from the QSPN1 SPORTS FOCAS show, ok? The crew wrote that into its notes. The next explainer used those voices without anyone asking. If you've said the same thing twice, it belongs in the notes. Lesson 3 shows you how.

5. Keep the keys. Some things never leave your hands: passwords (you type them), money (you approve every spend), and going public (your word). Write that list down before you start, and tell the crew. Lesson 10 builds it in for good.

5. The trap: a "thank you" that sends nothing

A signup form can show "Thanks, you're in!" and deliver nothing at all. Some form services need a one-time activation before they forward a single signup, and until then every signup disappears. The page still says thanks. Nobody knows until they wonder why nobody signed up.

How you beat it:

  1. The page only says "you're on the list" when the service confirms it. Anything else shows an error.
  2. Break it on purpose. Test the refusal and the dropped connection before a real person ever sees them.
  3. Do one real signup and find it in the real inbox. The proof is the email, not the thank-you.

The same rule covers every lesson in this course: ask the thing itself, not the report about it.

6. Your turn

  1. Write one outcome sentence for something small you want built: a page, a flyer, a 30-second video. Use the three parts: what, who, and what "done" looks like.
  2. Pick your go-live word. The exact sentence you'll say before anything goes public.
  3. Write your keys list. What your crew never does without you: money, passwords, posting, deleting.
  4. Bonus: find one thing you've corrected twice this month. That's your first note for Lesson 3.
